Vectors & geometric intuition

Coordinates, linear combinations, lengths, angles and linear maps

01 · Scalars, vectors and linear combinations

A scalar is one number; a vector is an object that can be added and scaled. In R² or R³, draw a vector as an arrow from the origin. Its coordinates describe it relative to an ordered basis. A point and a displacement can have the same coordinate list, but their roles differ.

Add vectors component by component and multiply each component by a scalar. The linear combination a u + b v scales two arrows and adds them head-to-tail. Negative coefficients reverse a direction. A zero coefficient omits a vector; the zero vector is always a linear combination.

The span of a set is every vector obtainable by its linear combinations. One nonzero vector spans a line through the origin. Two nonparallel vectors in R² span the entire plane. Two parallel vectors still span only a line, no matter how many times they are repeated.

Translate a geometric question into equations in the coefficients. To determine whether w is in span(u,v), solve a u+b v=w. This is the first bridge between geometry and a system of linear equations. You do not need calculus for any of these operations.

a u + b v = (a u₁+b v₁, …, a uₙ+b vₙ)ᵀ

Work it through

  1. Let u=(1,2), v=(2,−1). Find 3u+2v.
  2. 3u=(3,6) and 2v=(4,−2).
  3. Add components: 3u+2v=(7,4).
  4. Conversely, solve a+2b=7 and 2a−b=4 to recover a=3,b=2.
Application: Feature vectors, forces and image pixels all use weighted combinations. A linear prediction is a weighted combination of input features.
Watch the trap: A line not passing through the origin is an affine set, not a vector subspace.

02 · Dot products, norms and angles

For real coordinate vectors, uᵀv is the sum of products of corresponding components. The Euclidean norm is the square root of uᵀu. It measures length and is zero only for the zero vector. The 1-norm sums absolute values; the infinity norm is the largest absolute component.

The dot product equals ||u|| ||v|| cos θ for nonzero vectors. Positive means an acute angle, negative an obtuse angle, and zero means orthogonality. The angle of the zero vector is undefined even though its dot product with every vector is zero.

Cauchy–Schwarz bounds |uᵀv| by ||u|| ||v||. Equality holds when the vectors are linearly dependent, including the zero cases. The triangle inequality says ||u+v||≤||u||+||v||. These inequalities are useful for checking whether a proposed numeric answer is even possible.

Normalize a nonzero vector by dividing it by its norm. Normalization changes length to one without changing direction. It does not make two arbitrary vectors orthogonal. In complex vector spaces replace transpose with conjugate transpose in the standard inner product; the core examples here are real.

uᵀv = Σᵢuᵢvᵢ · ||u||₂ = √(uᵀu) · cos θ = uᵀv/(||u||₂||v||₂)

Work it through

  1. Take u=(3,4),v=(4,−3).
  2. uᵀv=12−12=0, so the vectors are perpendicular.
  3. Both norms are 5. Their normalized versions are (0.6,0.8) and (0.8,−0.6).
  4. For w=(1,1), ||w||₁=2, ||w||₂=√2 and ||w||∞=1.
Application: Cosine similarity compares directions of document or embedding vectors; Euclidean distance compares points by ||x−y||₂.
Watch the trap: Orthogonal means dot product zero. Orthonormal additionally requires unit length.

03 · Linear transformations and the column picture

A linear map T preserves addition and scaling: T(a u+b v)=aT(u)+bT(v). Consequently T(0)=0. A map can preserve zero without being linear, so use both defining properties. Translation by a nonzero vector is affine rather than linear.

A matrix stores the images of basis vectors as its columns. For A with columns a₁,…,aₙ, Ax=x₁a₁+…+xₙaₙ. An m×n matrix maps Rⁿ into Rᵐ. The output dimension comes from the number of rows, not columns.

A 2D rotation, reflection, scaling or shear can be linear. A singular map flattens some nonzero direction to zero. Matrix multiplication corresponds to composing maps: AB applies B first and then A to a column vector. Reversing that order generally changes the result.

Use the images of (1,0) and (0,1) to predict an entire grid transformation. Straight lines remain straight or collapse to points; parallel lines remain parallel or can collapse. This geometric view makes determinants, eigenvectors and singular values easier to understand later.

A=[T(e₁) … T(eₙ)] ⇒ Ax=ΣᵢxᵢT(eᵢ) · (AB)x=A(Bx)

Work it through

  1. Let A=[[1,1],[0,1]]. Its columns are (1,0) and (1,1).
  2. For x=(2,3), Ax=2(1,0)+3(1,1)=(5,3).
  3. Horizontal coordinates shift by the vertical coordinate: this is a shear.
  4. The origin is fixed and det(A)=1, so area is preserved.
Application: Coordinate changes in graphics and linear feature transformations in data pipelines use the same column picture.
Watch the trap: The rightmost matrix acts first when transformations act on column vectors.

Spaces, subspaces & bases

Axioms, closure tests, independence, dimension and coordinates

01 · Vector spaces and the subspace test

A vector space is a set with addition and scalar multiplication satisfying the vector-space axioms: associativity and commutativity of addition, a zero vector, additive inverses, the two distributive laws, associative scalar action and multiplication by scalar 1. Scalars come from a specified field, usually R in this guide.

Examples include Rⁿ, all m×n real matrices, polynomials of degree at most d, and real-valued functions on a fixed domain. A polynomial is a vector of coefficients in a chosen basis. The zero polynomial belongs to the space even though its degree is usually treated specially.

For a subset W of an existing vector space, check that W is nonempty and is closed under a u+b v for every u,v in W and scalars a,b. Equivalently check zero, addition and scalar multiplication. One counterexample disproves a subspace; checking a few successful examples cannot prove closure.

A homogeneous equation such as x+2y−z=0 defines a subspace because linear combinations preserve the zero right side. The equation x+2y−z=1 is an affine plane and fails the zero test. Intersections of subspaces are subspaces. Their union is a subspace only when one contains the other.

W≤V ⇔ W≠∅ and a u+b v∈W for every u,v∈W and a,b∈R

Work it through

  1. Let W={(x,y,z):x+2y−z=0}.
  2. Solve for x: x=−2y+z.
  3. Every vector is y(−2,1,0)+z(1,0,1).
  4. W is a plane through zero, spanned by those two independent vectors.
Application: The solutions of homogeneous constraints form feasible directions; null spaces are a central example.
Watch the trap: “Degree exactly d” is not a polynomial vector space: zero is excluded and cancellation can lower degree.

02 · Dependence, independence and redundancy

Vectors v₁,…,vₖ are independent when c₁v₁+…+cₖvₖ=0 has only the all-zero coefficient solution. Dependence means a nontrivial coefficient choice gives zero. In a dependent finite set, at least one vector can be written as a combination of the others.

Place the vectors as columns in a matrix A. Independence becomes the statement that Ax=0 has only x=0. Row reduction locates pivot columns; every column must be a pivot for column independence. More than n vectors in Rⁿ are always dependent.

Any set containing the zero vector is dependent. Two nonzero vectors are dependent exactly when they are scalar multiples. For three or more vectors, pairwise nonparallel directions do not guarantee independence: three vectors in a plane are necessarily dependent.

Removing a dependent vector does not shrink the span if it is a combination of the retained vectors. Adding a vector outside the span increases dimension by one. These observations explain why feature redundancy lowers matrix rank and why choosing a basis removes redundant descriptions.

[v₁ … vₖ]c=0 has only c=0 ⇔ {v₁,…,vₖ} is independent

Work it through

  1. Let v₁=(1,0),v₂=(0,1),v₃=(1,1).

  3. All three pairs are nonparallel, but the three-vector set is dependent.
  4. Keep v₁,v₂ to preserve the same span R² without redundancy.
Application: Duplicate or linearly redundant predictors make least-squares coefficients nonunique unless additional choices are imposed.
Watch the trap: Independent rows and independent columns have different counts; for a rectangular matrix, say which independence you mean.

03 · Basis, dimension and change of coordinates

A basis is an independent spanning set. Every vector in the space has a unique coordinate representation in that basis: spanning gives existence, independence gives uniqueness. The number of basis vectors is the dimension; all bases of the same finite-dimensional space have the same size.

The standard basis of Rⁿ consists of coordinate unit vectors. P_d has basis 1,x,…,xᵈ and dimension d+1. The space of m×n matrices has dimension mn. A basis of a subspace of Rⁿ may have fewer than n vectors.

If B is a square matrix whose columns are a basis, actual coordinates and basis coordinates satisfy x=B[x]_B. Solve Bc=x to obtain c=[x]_B. For two bases B and C, [x]_C=C⁻¹B[x]_B. The vector itself has not changed, only its description.

For subspaces U,W of a finite-dimensional V, dim(U+W)=dim U+dim W−dim(U∩W). A direct sum has intersection {0}, giving unique decomposition and adding dimensions. Coordinate transformations and rank arguments use this counting relationship repeatedly.

x=B[x]_B · [x]_C=C⁻¹B[x]_B · dim(U+W)=dim U+dim W−dim(U∩W)

Work it through

  1. Choose B=[(1,1),(1,−1)] and x=(4,2).
  2. The coordinate equations are c₁+c₂=4 and c₁−c₂=2.
  3. Therefore c₁=3,c₂=1, so [x]_B=(3,1).
  4. The standard coordinates (4,2) and B-coordinates (3,1) describe the same vector.
Application: Eigenvector bases and singular-vector bases simplify a transformation by choosing useful coordinates.
Watch the trap: An independent set need not span the whole ambient space; specify the space for which it is a basis.

Matrix operations & blocks

Dimensions, products, transpose, inverse and partitioned matrices

01 · Matrix arithmetic without shape mistakes

An m×n matrix has m rows and n columns. Addition requires the same shape and is componentwise. Scalar multiplication scales every entry. For A of shape m×n and B of shape n×p, AB has shape m×p; the inner dimensions must agree.

Each entry (AB)ᵢⱼ is a dot product of row i of A with column j of B. Equivalently, each output column is A times the corresponding column of B. Matrix multiplication is associative and distributive, but generally not commutative.

The identity I has ones on the diagonal and acts as a neutral transformation. The zero matrix sends every vector to zero. A product AB can be zero even when both factors are nonzero, because B’s outputs may lie in A’s null space.

In NumPy, @ means matrix multiplication; * means entrywise multiplication for arrays. A 1D array has no distinct row/column orientation, so use explicit reshaping when a calculation needs an n×1 column or 1×n row. Check shapes before checking arithmetic.

(AB)ᵢⱼ=ΣₖAᵢₖBₖⱼ · (m×n)(n×p)→m×p

Work it through

  1. A=[[1,2],[0,1]], B=[[2,0],[1,3]].
  2. AB first row is (1·2+2·1,1·0+2·3)=(4,6).
  3. AB second row is (1,3), so AB=[[4,6],[1,3]].
  4. BA=[[2,4],[1,5]], confirming that order matters.
Application: A batch of observations multiplied by a weight matrix is the same operation as many weighted feature combinations.

02 · Transpose, inverse and useful identities

The transpose exchanges rows and columns: (Aᵀ)ᵢⱼ=Aⱼᵢ. It reverses a product: (AB)ᵀ=BᵀAᵀ. The trace is the sum of diagonal entries of a square matrix. For compatible products, tr(AB)=tr(BA); this does not mean AB=BA.

A square matrix is invertible when there is a matrix A⁻¹ with AA⁻¹=A⁻¹A=I. Invertibility is equivalent to full rank, nonzero determinant, a zero-dimensional null space and a unique solution Ax=b for every b. A rectangular matrix has no two-sided inverse of this kind.

For invertible A and B, (AB)⁻¹=B⁻¹A⁻¹. Also (Aᵀ)⁻¹=(A⁻¹)ᵀ. There is no general identity (A+B)⁻¹=A⁻¹+B⁻¹. To solve Ax=b numerically, prefer a direct solve or factorization over explicitly forming the inverse.

For A=[[a,b],[c,d]], the inverse is [[d,−b],[−c,a]]/(ad−bc) when the denominator is nonzero. This formula is excellent for 2×2 exam arithmetic; it is not a recommendation to compute large inverses by cofactors.

(AB)ᵀ=BᵀAᵀ · (AB)⁻¹=B⁻¹A⁻¹ · A⁻¹=(1/(ad−bc))[[d,−b],[−c,a]]

Work it through

  1. A=[[2,1],[1,1]] has determinant 1.

  4. Verify by multiplying A(1,1)=(3,2), rather than trusting the formula alone.

03 · Partitioned matrices and Schur complements

A partitioned or block matrix groups rows and columns into submatrices. The underlying matrix is unchanged. Block addition and multiplication use the same rules as scalar entries, but each block product must have compatible dimensions and its order must be preserved.

For [[A,B],[C,D]] times [[E,F],[G,H]], the upper-left block is AE+BG and the other blocks follow row-by-column multiplication. Block-diagonal matrices act independently on coordinate groups. Their determinant is the product of block determinants.

When A is invertible, eliminate C by block row operations. The remaining lower-right block is S=D−CA⁻¹B, called the Schur complement of A. The determinant identity det(M)=det(A)det(S) follows. It requires A invertible in this form; do not use A⁻¹ when A is singular.

If both A and S are invertible, solve block equations by substitution: y=S⁻¹(g−CA⁻¹f), then x=A⁻¹(f−By). A block inverse exists and can be built from these expressions. Directly replacing det([[A,B],[C,D]]) with det(AD−BC) is not generally valid for arbitrary noncommuting blocks.

S=D−CA⁻¹B · det([[A,B],[C,D]])=det(A)det(S) [A invertible]

Work it through

  1. For M=[[2,1],[1,3]], use scalar blocks A=2,B=C=1,D=3.
  2. S=3−1·(1/2)·1=2.5.
  3. det(M)=2·2.5=5, matching 2·3−1·1.
  4. For block sizes larger than one, the multiplication order in CA⁻¹B becomes essential.
Application: Block elimination appears when separating parameter groups in least squares and covariance calculations.
Watch the trap: A partition is a grouping device; it does not make matrix blocks commute.
import numpy as np

01 · Symmetric, skew-symmetric and triangular matrices

A real square matrix is symmetric if Aᵀ=A and skew-symmetric if Aᵀ=−A. A skew-symmetric real matrix has zero diagonal, because aᵢᵢ=−aᵢᵢ. Every real square matrix splits uniquely into a symmetric and a skew-symmetric part.

A diagonal matrix has no off-diagonal entries. Upper triangular means entries below the diagonal are zero; lower triangular is the reverse. Products of upper triangular matrices remain upper triangular. A triangular matrix’s determinant and eigenvalues come directly from its diagonal.

A real symmetric matrix has real eigenvalues and an orthonormal eigenbasis. Products of two symmetric matrices need not be symmetric: AB is symmetric exactly when AB=BA for symmetric A,B. Distinguish a condition on each factor from a condition on their product.

02 · Orthogonal matrices preserve geometry

A real square matrix Q is orthogonal when QᵀQ=I. Its columns form an orthonormal basis, and its rows do too. Q⁻¹=Qᵀ. A rectangular matrix may have orthonormal columns, but is not an orthogonal square matrix under this convention.

Orthogonal transformations preserve dot products: (Qx)ᵀ(Qy)=xᵀy. Consequently they preserve Euclidean lengths, distances and angles. Rotations and reflections are examples. Orthogonal matrices can have negative entries; “orthogonal” is not a statement about all entries being zero except the diagonal.

Taking determinants of QᵀQ=I gives det(Q)²=1, so det(Q)=±1. A positive determinant preserves orientation; a negative determinant reverses it. Real eigenvalues of an orthogonal matrix can only be ±1, while complex eigenvalues have magnitude one.

03 · Idempotent matrices and projection matrices

An idempotent matrix satisfies P²=P: applying it twice has the same effect as applying it once. Its image consists of fixed vectors, and its kernel consists of vectors sent to zero. Every vector x decomposes as Px+(I−P)x into image and kernel components.

For an eigenpair Pv=λv, idempotence gives λ²=λ, hence λ is 0 or 1. Because the polynomial t(t−1) has distinct roots, an idempotent matrix is diagonalizable. Its trace equals its rank. I−P is also idempotent and projects onto the complementary component.

A real orthogonal projection onto a subspace is both symmetric and idempotent. An idempotent matrix that is not symmetric is an oblique projection: its residual need not be perpendicular to its image. Some questions use “projection matrix” broadly; state whether orthogonal projection is intended.

For a unit vector u, P=uuᵀ projects onto its line. For orthonormal columns U, P=UUᵀ projects onto their span. If the columns of A are independent but not orthonormal, P=A(AᵀA)⁻¹Aᵀ. A rank-deficient A requires a pseudoinverse or an independent basis instead.

Systems & Gaussian elimination

Augmented matrices, pivots, consistency and parametric solutions

01 · Translate equations into Ax=b

A system of m linear equations in n unknowns is Ax=b, where each row stores one equation’s coefficients. A solution makes all equations true simultaneously. Geometrically in R², two nonparallel lines intersect once, parallel distinct lines never meet, and coincident lines have infinitely many common points.

The column picture says b must belong to the column space of A. The augmented matrix [A|b] records both coefficients and the right side. Elementary row operations—swapping rows, scaling a row by a nonzero scalar and adding a multiple of another row—preserve the solution set.

A homogeneous system has b=0 and always admits x=0. It has nonzero solutions exactly when its columns are dependent, or equivalently rank(A)<n. For a nonhomogeneous consistent system, the general solution is x_p+z with z in the null space.

The number of equations alone does not determine the number of solutions. Dependent or contradictory equations can appear in square, tall or wide systems. Count pivots and test consistency instead of assuming “more equations means no solution”.

02 · Gaussian elimination, echelon form and pivoting

Gaussian elimination uses a pivot to remove entries below it, producing row echelon form. Leading nonzero entries move right as you move down rows; zero rows go to the bottom. Back substitution then determines pivot variables from the last nonzero row upward.

Reduced row echelon form (RREF) additionally makes each pivot equal to one and the only nonzero entry in its column. RREF is unique, although the sequence of row operations is not. Ordinary echelon form is not unique. Free columns identify variables that may be chosen independently in a consistent system.

If a pivot position is zero, search lower rows for a nonzero entry and swap; if none exists, skip that column. Numerical partial pivoting chooses a largest-magnitude available entry in the column to reduce instability from division by tiny values. In exact hand arithmetic, keep fractions rather than rounding early.

Dense elimination is O(n³) for a general square system; back substitution in an already triangular system is O(n²). Row operations preserve rank and solutions, but not determinant without tracking the operation’s effect. They also do not generally preserve eigenvalues.

Rank, nullity & fundamental spaces

Pivot counting, kernels, column spaces and dimension identities

01 · Rank–nullity and the four fundamental spaces

The column space of an m×n matrix is a subspace of Rᵐ; the row space is a subspace of Rⁿ. Both have dimension rank(A)=r. The null space consists of x in Rⁿ with Ax=0. The left null space consists of y in Rᵐ with Aᵀy=0.

Rank–nullity says dim Null(A)=n−r. Applying it to Aᵀ gives dim Null(Aᵀ)=m−r. Domain dimension is split into independent input directions that survive and directions collapsed to zero. Rank cannot exceed min(m,n).

Row reduction produces a row-space basis from the nonzero echelon rows. To obtain a column-space basis, identify pivot columns in the reduced matrix but select those columns from the original matrix. Row operations change the actual column vectors, even though they preserve their dependence relationships.

Rows are perpendicular to every null-space vector, so Row(A)=Null(A)⊥. Likewise Col(A)=Null(Aᵀ)⊥. The ambient spaces matter: a row-space vector has n entries while a column-space vector has m entries. Do not confuse their equal dimensions with equal sets.

02 · Rank inequalities and invertible transformations

Multiplication by an invertible matrix on either side preserves rank. Row operations are multiplication by invertible elementary matrices, explaining rank preservation. Transposition also preserves rank. Multiplication by a singular matrix can reduce rank but cannot increase it beyond the factors’ ranks.

For compatible A and B, rank(AB)≤min(rank A,rank B). If A is m×n and B is n×p, Sylvester’s lower bound is rank(AB)≥rank A+rank B−n. Also rank(A+B)≤rank A+rank B for same-shaped matrices.

For real A, Null(AᵀA)=Null(A), since xᵀAᵀAx=||Ax||². Therefore rank(AᵀA)=rank(A). Full column rank is equivalent to AᵀA being positive definite and invertible. This argument relies on the conjugate transpose in the complex case.

Rank is exact in algebra but tolerance-dependent in floating-point software. A tiny singular value may be treated as numerically zero. Use exact symbolic reasoning for exam matrices; in data analysis inspect singular values, scaling and the tolerance rather than treating a reported rank as an absolute physical fact.

03 · Orthogonal complements and solvability certificates

The orthogonal complement W⊥ contains vectors perpendicular to every vector of W. In Rⁿ, dim W+dim W⊥=n and W∩W⊥={0}. Every vector has a unique orthogonal decomposition into a component in W and one in W⊥.

A single y with Aᵀy=0 but yᵀb≠0 certifies inconsistency. It represents a linear combination of left-side equations cancelling to zero while the same combination of right sides does not. It is a compact proof of contradiction.

Determinants & invertibility

Area, row-operation rules, cofactors and parameter cases

01 · Determinants as signed volume scaling

The determinant of a square matrix is its signed volume scale factor. In 2D, |det A| is the area of the parallelogram formed by its columns. A negative determinant reverses orientation. A zero determinant means the columns collapse volume into a lower-dimensional set.

For [[a,b],[c,d]], det A=ad−bc. In higher dimensions, the determinant is alternating and multilinear in the columns (or rows), normalized so det I=1. Swapping two columns changes its sign; equal or dependent columns make it zero.

A triangular matrix has determinant equal to the product of its diagonal entries. A square matrix is invertible exactly when its determinant is nonzero. A rectangular matrix does not have an ordinary determinant, even though its columns can span a volume through a Gram determinant.

Determinant is not a measure of all conditioning effects. A matrix can have determinant 1 yet stretch one direction by 10⁶ and compress another by 10⁻⁶. Singular values describe the directional effects more fully.

  4. If the second column were twice the first, the parallelogram would collapse and the determinant would be zero.

Cramer’s rule gives xᵢ=det(Aᵢ)/det A for a nonsingular square system, replacing column i by b. It is useful for proofs and small symbolic questions, but elimination is usually more efficient for large numerical systems.

Orthogonality & least squares

Projections, Gram–Schmidt, QR and the best-fit interpretation

01 · Project a vector onto a line or subspace

The orthogonal projection of b onto a nonzero vector a is p=a(aᵀb)/(aᵀa). The scalar coefficient is chosen so that residual b−p is perpendicular to a. The projection p lies on the line, and the residual measures the shortest displacement from b to that line.

For an orthonormal basis q₁,…,qᵣ of a subspace, add the line projections: p=Σqᵢ(qᵢᵀb). With Q holding those columns, p=QQᵀb. For an arbitrary independent basis A, solve AᵀA c=Aᵀb and set p=Ac.

The Pythagorean identity ||b||²=||p||²+||b−p||² holds because the components are perpendicular. For any other point p+w in the subspace, its squared error is ||b−p||²+||w||², proving the closest point is unique.

The projection vector is unique even if the spanning columns are redundant. The coefficient representation may then be nonunique. Use an independent basis, QR/SVD or the pseudoinverse; do not invert a singular Gram matrix.

p=a(aᵀb)/(aᵀa) · P=A(AᵀA)⁻¹Aᵀ when A has independent columns

  3. The residual is (2,−1), with aᵀr=2−2=0.
  4. The squared lengths are 10=5+5.
Application: Denoising by subspace projection retains representable structure and removes the orthogonal residual.

02 · Gram–Schmidt and QR factorization

Gram–Schmidt turns independent vectors into an orthonormal basis for the same span. Normalize the first vector. For the next vector, subtract its projection on every earlier orthonormal direction, then normalize what remains. A zero remainder signals dependence.

With independent columns a₁,…,aₙ, define rᵢⱼ=qᵢᵀaⱼ for i<j, subtract Σᵢ<ⱼrᵢⱼqᵢ, and let rⱼⱼ be the norm of the remainder. These coefficients form an upper-triangular matrix R and A=QR, where Q has orthonormal columns.

Modified Gram–Schmidt and Householder QR improve numerical stability relative to classical Gram–Schmidt. This guide uses the exact geometry for hand work and NumPy’s QR for computation. Signs of columns and corresponding rows can vary between valid QR factorizations.

For full-column-rank least squares, solve Rx̂=Qᵀb. This avoids explicitly forming AᵀA, which can worsen conditioning. QR is supporting depth connecting the named projections and systems topics; LU and SVD are explicitly named in the official syllabus.

vⱼ=aⱼ−Σᵢ<ⱼ(qᵢᵀaⱼ)qᵢ; qⱼ=vⱼ/||vⱼ||; A=QR

03 · Least squares from geometry to regression

When Ax=b is inconsistent, least squares chooses x̂ minimizing ||Ax−b||₂². The fitted vector Ax̂ is the orthogonal projection of b onto Col(A). Its residual is perpendicular to every column, yielding AᵀA x̂=Aᵀb, the normal equations.

If A has full column rank, AᵀA is invertible and x̂ is unique. If rank is deficient, fitted values are still unique but coefficients can differ by a null-space vector. The pseudoinverse selects the minimum-norm coefficient vector among least-squares solutions.

For a line y≈β₀+β₁t, put a column of ones and a column of t values into A. The intercept is then an ordinary coefficient. If the model omits the intercept, the fitted line is forced through the origin; this changes both the meaning and the solution.

Calculus gives the same result by differentiating the quadratic objective: gradient 2Aᵀ(Ax−b)=0. The projection derivation does not require calculus; use the linked gradient bridge when you want to connect the two viewpoints. Numerically prefer QR/SVD-based solvers over explicit normal-equation inversion.

Eigenvalues & eigenvectors

Invariant directions, multiplicities, diagonalization and spectral reasoning

01 · Find eigenvalues and eigenspaces

An eigenvector v≠0 satisfies Av=λv: its direction is unchanged apart from stretching or reversal. The zero vector is never an eigenvector. Eigenvalues are defined for square matrices. An eigenvalue may be zero, corresponding to a nonzero vector in the null space.

Solve det(A−λI)=0 to find candidate eigenvalues, then solve (A−λI)v=0 for each eigenspace. Using det(λI−A) changes the polynomial by a sign in odd dimensions but leaves its roots unchanged. An eigenspace includes zero, even though eigenvectors exclude zero.

The trace equals the sum of eigenvalues and determinant their product, counting algebraic multiplicities over the complex numbers. A triangular matrix’s eigenvalues are its diagonal entries. A real matrix can have complex eigenvalues, for example a 90° planar rotation.

Eigenvectors for distinct eigenvalues are independent. Their lengths are arbitrary; any nonzero scalar multiple remains an eigenvector. A numerical library may choose different signs or normalizations, so verify Av=λv rather than comparing one expected printed vector exactly.

  4. Normalize these vectors if an orthonormal eigenbasis is needed.

02 · Multiplicity and diagonalization

Algebraic multiplicity counts how often an eigenvalue occurs as a characteristic-polynomial root. Geometric multiplicity is the dimension of its eigenspace, equal to nullity(A−λI). For each eigenvalue, geometric multiplicity lies between 1 and its algebraic multiplicity.

A square n×n matrix is diagonalizable over a field when it has n independent eigenvectors over that field. Put them in S; then A=SΛS⁻¹. Distinct eigenvalues guarantee diagonalizability, but are not necessary: I has a repeated eigenvalue and an entire space of eigenvectors.

The matrix [[1,1],[0,1]] has eigenvalue 1 twice but only one independent eigenvector, so it is not diagonalizable. Repeated roots require an eigenspace calculation. A real rotation may be diagonalizable over C while not over R, so state the field.

03 · Powers, similarity and eigenvalue shortcuts

If Av=λv, then Aᵏv=λᵏv for nonnegative integers k. More generally p(A)v=p(λ)v for a polynomial p. If A is invertible, A⁻¹v=(1/λ)v. These facts transform eigenvalues without recalculating a full determinant.

Similar matrices B=S⁻¹AS represent the same linear map in different bases. They share characteristic polynomial, eigenvalues with multiplicities, determinant, trace and rank. Similarity is not the same as a row operation or a congruence QᵀAQ.

Cayley–Hamilton says a matrix satisfies its characteristic polynomial. For a 2×2 matrix, A²−tr(A)A+det(A)I=0. This reduces high powers to lower powers and can express an inverse when the determinant is nonzero.

For a diagonalizable matrix, Aᵏ=SΛᵏS⁻¹. Modes with |λ|<1 decay under repeated application; |λ|>1 modes grow. At |λ|=1, a defective matrix may still have polynomial growth, so eigenvalue magnitudes alone need care on the boundary. This dynamical viewpoint is advanced supporting depth.

Quadratic forms & curvature

Definiteness, principal axes, Rayleigh quotients and quadratic optimization

01 · Reading and classifying a quadratic form

A real quadratic form is q(x)=xᵀAx. Only the symmetric part S=(A+Aᵀ)/2 matters: the skew part contributes zero. Write each cross term across two symmetric entries. Thus 2x²+6xy+5y² corresponds to [[2,3],[3,5]], not [[2,6],[6,5]]. A linear or constant term belongs to a quadratic function but not to its homogeneous quadratic form.

For symmetric A, positive definite means xᵀAx>0 for every nonzero x; positive semidefinite permits zero. Negative definite and negative semidefinite reverse these inequalities. Indefinite means both signs occur. The zero vector never disproves definiteness because the definition explicitly excludes it.

The spectral theorem gives q(x)=Σ λᵢzᵢ² in orthonormal eigenvector coordinates. Consequently all eigenvalues positive means positive definite; all nonnegative means positive semidefinite. Both positive and negative eigenvalues mean indefinite. A zero eigenvalue rules out strict definiteness, but does not by itself imply indefiniteness.

Sylvester’s criterion for a real symmetric matrix: positive definiteness is equivalent to all leading principal minors being positive. For positive semidefiniteness, all principal minors must be nonnegative; leading minors alone are insufficient. In 2D, a>0 and ac−b²>0 characterize positive definiteness of [[a,b],[b,c]].

02 · Principal axes and the Rayleigh quotient

A real symmetric matrix admits A=QΛQᵀ with Q orthogonal. Set x=Qz, equivalently z=Qᵀx. Orthogonal coordinates preserve lengths while removing cross terms: xᵀAx=zᵀΛz. This is a change of coordinates, not a row reduction.

For nonzero x, the Rayleigh quotient R(x)=xᵀAx/(xᵀx) is a weighted average of eigenvalues, with weights zᵢ²/Σzⱼ². It lies between the smallest and largest eigenvalues. On the unit sphere, the minimum and maximum of xᵀAx are those extreme eigenvalues, attained at corresponding eigenvectors.

An equation xᵀAx=1 with positive definite A describes an ellipsoid. Along eigenvector i, the semiaxis length is 1/√λᵢ. Large curvature means a shorter axis. If eigenvalues have mixed signs the geometry is hyperbolic; zero eigenvalues produce unconstrained flat directions.

Orthogonal diagonalization is a similarity and also a congruence because Q⁻¹=Qᵀ. General congruence SᵀAS preserves the counts of positive, negative and zero squares (inertia), but need not preserve eigenvalues. Treat this distinction as supporting depth for reasoning about quadratic forms.

03 · Quadratic minimization and least-squares curvature

Consider f(x)=½xᵀAx−bᵀx+c with A symmetric. Its gradient is Ax−b and its Hessian is A. These are multivariable supporting concepts; core single-variable differentiation is taught in the calculus recap. The linear algebra of solving Ax=b already gives stationary points.

If A is positive definite, x*=A⁻¹b is the unique global minimizer. Completing the square proves f(x)=f(x*)+½(x−x*)ᵀA(x−x*). Prefer solving the system numerically instead of explicitly computing the inverse.

If A is positive semidefinite and b lies in its column space, minimizers exist but can be nonunique along null directions. If b is outside that column space, the linear term makes f unbounded below along a null direction. An indefinite A also makes the unconstrained function unbounded below.

LU, pivoting & stable solves

Elimination factors, triangular substitution and numerical judgment

01 · Elimination becomes LU decomposition

Gaussian elimination subtracts multiples of pivot rows. Store these multipliers below the diagonal of a unit lower triangular L; the resulting upper triangular matrix is U. Without row swaps, the factorization is A=LU. It packages elimination so many right-hand sides can reuse the same work.

For the Doolittle convention L has ones on its diagonal. At step k, multiplier lᵢk is the current entry aᵢk divided by pivot aₖk; subtract lᵢk times row k. Previously stored multipliers belong to L rather than the current U. A zero pivot can prevent this unpivoted construction even when A is invertible.

For an n×n matrix with all leading principal minors nonzero, an LU factorization with unit lower L exists and is unique. This is a useful sufficient condition in the nonsingular setting. Singular matrices can sometimes have LU too, but diagonal pivots may be zero and uniqueness or standard elimination can fail.

Once A=LU, solve Ly=b and then Ux=y. Determinants become the product of U diagonal entries because det(L)=1. Dense factorization costs O(n³), while the pair of triangular solves costs O(n²) per right-hand side.

02 · Permutation matrices and partial pivoting

A permutation matrix reorders rows when multiplied on the left. A row swap changes determinant sign; every permutation matrix is orthogonal. With pivoting, this guide uses PA=LU. Always check the convention: some software reports A=PLU, where its P is the inverse of ours.

Partial pivoting chooses the largest absolute entry in the current column among the remaining rows. It avoids a zero pivot when a usable one exists and reduces growth from dividing by tiny numbers. It is not a guarantee of perfect numerical accuracy on every matrix, but is the standard practical default.

When later row swaps occur, swap the already-computed entries of those rows in L as well. Swapping only U would invalidate PA=LU. The right-hand side must follow the same permutation: solve Ly=Pb and then Ux=y.

03 · Cholesky, conditioning and residuals

For a real symmetric positive definite matrix, Cholesky gives A=LLᵀ with L lower triangular and positive diagonal. It uses symmetry and requires less work than general LU. SVD, pseudoinverse & low-rank models

Rectangular matrices, singular directions, minimum-norm solutions and compression

01 · Singular value decomposition from first principles

Every real m×n matrix has A=UΣVᵀ. In the full form, U is m×m orthogonal, V is n×n orthogonal, and Σ is m×n with nonnegative diagonal singular values sorted in descending order. Unlike eigendecomposition, SVD works for rectangular and defective matrices.

AᵀA is symmetric positive semidefinite. Its eigenvectors supply right singular directions vᵢ and its eigenvalues are σᵢ². For σᵢ>0, define uᵢ=Avᵢ/σᵢ. Complete orthonormal bases in the null spaces for zero singular values. AAᵀ has the same positive eigenvalues but may have a different number of zeros.

Geometrically, Vᵀ rotates or reflects the input, Σ scales along perpendicular axes (and may collapse directions), and U rotates or reflects the output. The image of the unit circle is an ellipse whose semiaxis lengths are the singular values. Eigenvectors and singular vectors have different meanings.

Let k=min(m,n). The economy form uses U of shape m×k, Σ k×k and Vᵀ k×n. The compact rank-r form keeps only positive singular values, with shapes m×r, r×r and r×n. State which meaning of thin SVD you use; libraries and textbooks differ. Signs of paired uᵢ,vᵢ can flip without changing A.

Avᵢ=σᵢuᵢ · σᵢ=√λᵢ(AᵀA) · rank(A)=#{σᵢ>0}

Work it through

  1. Take A=[[3,0],[0,2],[0,0]], a 3×2 matrix.
  2. AᵀA=diag(9,4), giving singular values 3 and 2.
  3. Right directions are e₁,e₂; corresponding left directions are the first two axes in R³.
  4. The economy shapes are U:3×2, Σ:2×2, Vᵀ:2×2.
Application: SVD describes how a data matrix stretches directions even when observations and features have different counts.
Watch the trap: Singular values are nonnegative; they are not generally the eigenvalues or absolute diagonal entries of A.

np.set_printoptions(precision=4, suppress=True)
A=np.array([[3.,0.],[0.,2.],[0.,0.]])

02 · Pseudoinverse and minimum-norm least squares

The Moore–Penrose pseudoinverse is A⁺=VΣ⁺Uᵀ: invert each positive singular value and transpose the rectangular diagonal shape, leaving zeros at zero. It exists for every matrix, equals A⁻¹ when an ordinary inverse exists, and can solve singular or rectangular systems.

x*=A⁺b minimizes ||Ax−b||₂. Among all least-squares minimizers, it has the smallest Euclidean norm. Every least-squares minimizer has the form A⁺b+z with z in null(A). If Ax=b is consistent, the same formula describes exact solutions.

AA⁺ is the orthogonal projector onto col(A); A⁺A projects onto row(A). The four defining relations are AA⁺A=A, A⁺AA⁺=A⁺, (AA⁺)ᵀ=AA⁺, and (A⁺A)ᵀ=A⁺A. For full column rank, A⁺=(AᵀA)⁻¹Aᵀ; for full row rank, A⁺=Aᵀ(AAᵀ)⁻¹.

Tiny singular values can amplify noise when inverted. Numerical routines use a tolerance to decide which directions to discard. This trades exact fit for stability. Rank and pseudoinverse answers near the tolerance boundary depend on scaling and the chosen cutoff.

A⁺=VΣ⁺Uᵀ · xLS=A⁺b · AA⁺=Pcol(A) · A⁺A=Prow(A)

Work it through

  1. Solve x₁+x₂=2, so A=[1,1] and b=2.
  2. The pseudoinverse is [1/2,1/2]ᵀ, giving x*=(1,1).
  3. All solutions are (1+t,1−t). Their squared norms are 2+2t².
  4. The unique smallest norm occurs at t=0.
Application: Minimum-norm fitting handles redundant features and underdetermined inverse problems.
Watch the trap: A pseudoinverse returns a least-squares solution; it does not make an inconsistent system exact.
import numpy as np
np.set_printoptions(precision=4, suppress=True)
A=np.array([[1.,1.]])
print(np.linalg.pinv(A)@np.array([2.]))

03 · Truncated SVD and the bridge to PCA

Write A=Σᵢσᵢuᵢvᵢᵀ as a sum of rank-one outer products. Keeping the largest k singular values gives Aₖ, a best rank-at-most-k approximation in both spectral and Frobenius norms. This is the Eckart–Young result; uniqueness can fail when the cutoff singular values tie.

The spectral error is ||A−Aₖ||₂=σₖ₊₁. The squared Frobenius error is Σᵢ>k σᵢ². Total squared Frobenius norm is Σσᵢ², so retained energy is the fraction in the first k squared singular values. For exact rank r, keeping r terms reconstructs A exactly.

For PCA, arrange samples in rows of X and center each feature by subtracting its column mean. With n samples, sample covariance is XᵀX/(n−1). Right singular vectors are principal directions; covariance eigenvalues are σᵢ²/(n−1). Scores in k dimensions are XVₖ=UₖΣₖ.

Centering matters: uncentered SVD approximates the original matrix, whereas PCA describes variation about the mean. Standardizing features is a separate modeling choice because it changes the geometry. PCA and regularization here are applications of linear algebra; the complete machine-learning syllabus belongs to its own track.

Aₖ=Σᵢ≤k σᵢuᵢvᵢᵀ · ||A−Aₖ||F²=Σᵢ>k σᵢ² · PCA variance=σᵢ²/(n−1)

Work it through

  1. Suppose singular values are 5,3,1. Total squared energy is 25+9+1=35.
  2. The best rank-one squared Frobenius error is 9+1=10.
  3. Rank two retains 34/35 of the energy, approximately 97.14%.
  4. Its spectral error is 1 and its Frobenius error is also 1.
Application: Image compression, denoising and low-dimensional data visualization all use dominant singular directions.
Watch the trap: Energy uses squared singular values, not their sum. Center data before interpreting SVD directions as PCA axes.
import numpy as np
np.set_printoptions(precision=4, suppress=True)
A=np.diag([5.,3.,1.])
U,s,Vt=np.linalg.svd(A)
Ak=(U[:,:2]*s[:2])@Vt[:2]
print(Ak)
print(np.linalg.norm(A-Ak,'fro'))

1. Singular values are square roots of eigenvalues of…

  2. A always
  3. A+Aᵀ always
  4. A⁻¹
Answer & reasoning

AᵀA. AᵀA is symmetric positive semidefinite.

2. An m×n matrix has a full SVD with U shape…

  1. n×n
  2. m×n always
  3. 1×m
  4. m×m
Answer & reasoning

m×m. U is an orthonormal basis of the output space.

3. Rank equals the number of…

  1. Nonzero entries
  2. Distinct singular values
  3. Positive singular values
  4. Positive eigenvalues of A always
Answer & reasoning

Positive singular values. Count nonzero singular values with multiplicity.

4. SVD exists for…

  1. Only symmetric matrices
  2. Every real rectangular matrix
  3. Only square matrices
  4. Only invertible matrices
Answer & reasoning

Every real rectangular matrix. Rectangular or rank-deficient matrices are included.

5. For diag(−3,2), singular values are…

  1. 3,2
  2. −3,2
  3. 9,4
  4. 1/3,1/2
Answer & reasoning

3,2. They are nonnegative square roots of 9 and 4.

6. Pseudoinverse of diag(2,0) is…

  1. diag(1/2,∞)
  2. diag(2,0)
  3. Undefined
  4. diag(1/2,0)
Answer & reasoning

diag(1/2,0). Only positive singular values are inverted.

7. A⁺b selects which least-squares solution?

  1. An arbitrary random one
  2. Only an exact solution
  3. The minimum Euclidean norm one
  4. The maximum norm one
Answer & reasoning

The minimum Euclidean norm one. It discards the free null-space component.

8. For singular values 5,3,1, rank-one squared Frobenius error is…

  1. 1
  2. 10
  3. 4
  4. 9
Answer & reasoning

10. Sum squares of discarded singular values: 3²+1².

9. For singular values 4 and 0.5, κ₂ is…

  1. 8
  2. 4.5
  3. 2
  4. 0.125

8. The ratio of largest to smallest is 4/0.5.

10. Before interpreting SVD as covariance PCA, usually…

  1. Add a row of zeros only
  2. Normalize every singular value to one
  3. Transpose without centering
  4. Center feature columns
Answer & reasoning

Center feature columns. PCA variation is measured about the mean; scaling is a separate choice.
