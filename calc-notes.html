Functions & algebra refresh

Domains, graphs, composition, exponentials, logarithms and trigonometry

01 · Functions, domains and graphs

A real function assigns exactly one output to each input in its domain. The domain is the allowed input set, the codomain is the declared target set, and the range is the set actually attained. A graph plots pairs (x,f(x)); a vertical line can meet it at most once for each input.

Find a domain before manipulating a formula. A denominator cannot be zero, a real square root needs a nonnegative argument, and a logarithm needs a positive argument. Intersect all restrictions. Cancellation can simplify a formula without restoring an input excluded by the original expression.

A function is even if f(−x)=f(x), odd if f(−x)=−f(x), when both signs belong to the domain. f(x−a)+b shifts a graph right by a and up by b. A negative multiplier outside reflects it vertically. Piecewise formulas must specify which branch owns a boundary.

Read intervals carefully: open endpoints are excluded; closed endpoints are included. A maximum must be attained in the domain. The supremum is a least upper bound and need not be attained. This difference becomes essential in optimization on open or unbounded intervals.

Domain of √(x−1)/(x−3): [1,3) ∪ (3,∞)

Work it through

  1. For f(x)=(x²−1)/(x−1), x=1 is excluded.
  2. For x≠1, factor and cancel to obtain f(x)=x+1.
  3. The graph is the line y=x+1 with a hole at (1,2).
  4. Defining f(1)=2 would create a new, continuous extension.
Application: A model’s input constraints matter as much as its formula: negative counts or invalid logarithms can break a data pipeline.
Watch the trap: A simplified expression can have a larger domain than the original function.

02 · Composition, inverses and algebraic preparation

The composition (f∘g)(x)=f(g(x)) requires x in the domain of g and g(x) in the domain of f. Order matters. An inverse function reverses input and output, and exists on the full range when f is one-to-one. Restrict a domain when needed: x² is invertible on [0,∞), not on all real numbers.

An inverse f⁻¹ is not the reciprocal 1/f. Solve y=f(x) for x, then interchange names. A graph of an inverse reflects across y=x. Strict monotonicity is a convenient sufficient condition for being one-to-one on an interval.

Factor polynomials, combine rational fractions, and rationalize square-root differences before trying a limit. Identities such as a²−b²=(a−b)(a+b) and (a+b)²=a²+2ab+b² remove many apparent difficulties. Keep excluded points in the domain record.

An absolute value is piecewise: |x|=x for x≥0 and −x for x<0. Inequalities reverse when multiplying or dividing by a negative quantity. Squaring an equation can introduce extraneous solutions; check candidates in the original expression.

03 · Exponential, logarithmic and trigonometric essentials

For a>0 and a≠1, aˣ is positive and has inverse logₐx. The natural exponential eˣ and natural logarithm ln x are especially convenient for calculus. Laws include ln(ab)=ln a+ln b and ln(aʳ)=r ln a for a>0; there is no corresponding sum law ln(a+b)=ln a+ln b.

The natural logarithm has domain x>0, range all real numbers, and grows slowly. eˣ has all real inputs and positive outputs. Exponentials eventually outgrow any fixed positive power of x, which eventually outgrows ln x as x→∞. These comparisons guide limit calculations.

Angles in calculus are measured in radians: a full turn is 2π. On the unit circle, cos x is horizontal and sin x is vertical. Recall sin²x+cos²x=1, tan x=sin x/cos x, sin(−x)=−sin x and cos(−x)=cos x.

Near zero, sin x behaves like x, cos x like 1−x²/2 and eˣ like 1+x. These are local approximations, not global identities. The derivative and Taylor lessons justify them and quantify where an approximation is useful.

Limits & continuity

One-sided behavior, standard limits, discontinuities and existence theorems

01 · What a limit really says

The statement limₓ→ₐ f(x)=L concerns values near a, with x≠a. It does not require f(a) to exist or equal L. Formally, for every ε>0 there is δ>0 so that 0<|x−a|<δ implies |f(x)−L|<ε. You control the output error by restricting the input neighborhood.

A two-sided limit exists when the left and right limits exist and are equal. Piecewise functions often require separate branch calculations. An infinite limit describes unbounded behavior; it is not a finite real limit. Oscillation can prevent a limit even when values remain bounded.

When finite limits exist, sums and products commute with limits; quotients do too if the denominator limit is nonzero. Direct substitution works for continuous formulas. Expressions 0/0 and ∞/∞ are indeterminate forms, not answers.

The squeeze theorem compares a function with two bounds having the same limit. For example, |x sin(1/x)|≤|x| proves its limit is zero at zero, even though sin(1/x) itself keeps oscillating. Numerical sampling supports intuition but cannot prove a limit.

limₓ→ₐ f(x)=L ⇔ f(x) approaches L from both sides of a

Work it through

  2. As x approaches 1 from either side, x+1 approaches 2.
  3. The limit is 2 even though the original expression is undefined at 1.
  4. For |x|/x, the left limit is −1 and right limit 1, so no two-sided limit exists.
Application: Limits define derivatives and justify approximations near a parameter value.
Watch the trap: f(a), limₓ→ₐf(x), and the existence of a limit are three separate questions.

02 · Standard limits and indeterminate forms

Memorize the foundational limits sin x/x→1, (1−cos x)/x²→1/2, (eˣ−1)/x→1 and ln(1+x)/x→1 as x→0, with radians for trigonometry. Substitute a scaled variable carefully: sin(3x)/x→3.

For rational functions at infinity, compare highest powers. Equal degrees give the ratio of leading coefficients; a larger denominator degree gives zero. A larger numerator degree needs sign and direction analysis. Rationalizing √(x²+x)−x for x→+∞ avoids subtracting two large terms.

L’Hôpital’s rule is conditional: for a 0/0 or ∞/∞ quotient with differentiable numerator and denominator near the point, nonzero denominator derivative, and an existing appropriate derivative-ratio limit, the original quotient has that limit. Transform products or powers into a suitable quotient first; do not apply the rule to an arbitrary fraction.

For forms such as 1^∞, take a logarithm: if y=g(x)^{h(x)}>0, study ln y=h(x)ln g(x), then exponentiate. For example (1+x)^{1/x}→e. Taylor expansion can offer an alternative once its validity and remainder are understood.

03 · Continuity and its consequences

Continuity at a means f(a) exists, the limit exists, and the limit equals f(a). At an included endpoint use the one-sided limit within the domain. Polynomials are continuous everywhere; rational functions are continuous where their denominators are nonzero.

A removable discontinuity has a finite limit but a missing or mismatched value. A jump has unequal finite one-sided limits. Infinite behavior and oscillatory behavior create other discontinuities. A piecewise function is continuous at a join only when both limiting branch values equal the assigned value.

The intermediate value theorem says a continuous function on [a,b] takes every value between f(a) and f(b). Opposite signs therefore ensure a root. It does not guarantee uniqueness; monotonicity can provide that extra conclusion.

The extreme value theorem guarantees a continuous function on a closed bounded interval attains a maximum and a minimum. Dropping continuity, closedness, or boundedness can break the guarantee. Differentiability implies continuity, but a continuous corner such as |x| is not differentiable at zero.

Continuous at a ⇔ limₓ→ₐf(x)=f(a) · continuous on [a,b] ⇒ extrema attained

  4. Thus k=1; this only establishes continuity, not matching derivatives.

Differentiation from scratch

Difference quotients, derivative rules, chain rule and higher derivatives

01 · Derivative as a limiting slope

The derivative f′(a) is limₕ→₀[f(a+h)−f(a)]/h when the finite two-sided limit exists. It is the tangent slope and instantaneous rate of change. The numerator is an output change; h is the input change. The derivative has units of output per input.

A secant joins two points, while a tangent is the limiting line at one point. Local linearization is f(a+h)≈f(a)+f′(a)h. Differentiability is stronger: the approximation error divided by |h| tends to zero. A vertical tangent may have unbounded slope and does not give a finite derivative.

At a piecewise join, check continuity first, then compare left and right derivative limits using the value at the join. For |x| at zero, left slope is −1 and right slope is 1. For x|x| both slopes at zero are 0, so the visual presence of an absolute value alone does not settle differentiability.

Finite differences approximate derivatives. A centered difference [f(a+h)−f(a−h)]/(2h) often has smaller truncation error than a forward difference for smooth f. Very tiny h can worsen floating-point cancellation; shrinking h is not an unlimited route to accuracy.

f′(a)=limₕ→₀ [f(a+h)−f(a)]/h · tangent: y=f(a)+f′(a)(x−a)

Work it through

  3. The limit is 4, so f′(2)=4.
  4. The tangent line is y=4+4(x−2)=4x−4.

02 · The derivative toolkit

For real powers where defined, d(xʳ)/dx=r xʳ⁻¹. A constant differentiates to zero; sums and constant multiples differentiate term by term. The product rule is (uv)′=u′v+uv′. The quotient rule is (u/v)′=(u′v−uv′)/v² where v≠0.

The basic derivatives are (eˣ)′=eˣ, (ln x)′=1/x for x>0, (sin x)′=cos x, (cos x)′=−sin x and (tan x)′=sec²x where defined. Also (aˣ)′=aˣ ln a and (logₐx)′=1/(x ln a), with a>0,a≠1.

Chain rule differentiates a composition: (f(g(x)))′=f′(g(x))g′(x). Work from outside to inside. For e^{x²}, retain the outer exponential and multiply by 2x. For ln|u(x)| where u≠0, the derivative is u′/u.

For inverse functions, (f⁻¹)′(y)=1/f′(x) at y=f(x) when f′(x)≠0 and a differentiable inverse exists locally. In particular, (arcsin x)′=1/√(1−x²) on (−1,1) and (arctan x)′=1/(1+x²). Do not confuse inverse trigonometric notation with reciprocal functions.

03 · Implicit, logarithmic and higher differentiation

Implicit differentiation treats y as a function of x. Differentiating x²+y²=1 gives 2x+2yy′=0, so y′=−x/y where y≠0. This formula is local on a branch, and fails to describe a finite slope where its denominator vanishes.

Logarithmic differentiation helps when both base and exponent vary. For y=xˣ on x>0, ln y=x ln x. Differentiate to get y′/y=ln x+1, hence y′=xˣ(ln x+1). A general u(x)^{v(x)} needs u>0 for this real-log method.

The second derivative differentiates the first derivative and measures change of slope. Higher derivatives repeat the operation. They power Taylor approximations. A derivative can exist without being continuous; higher differentiability is a further condition, not an automatic consequence.

For piecewise functions, matching values ensures continuity and matching first slopes ensures differentiability at the join. Matching second derivatives is a separate check. Some exam questions ask for parameter values satisfying more than one of these conditions.

y=uᵛ>0 ⇒ y′=uᵛ(v′ln u+v u′/u) · f⁽ⁿ⁾ = n-th derivative

Work it through

  1. For y=xˣ, take logs: ln y=x ln x.
  2. Differentiate: y′/y=ln x+1.
  3. Multiply by y: y′=xˣ(ln x+1).
  4. At x=1, y′=1.
Application: Implicit curves arise in constraints; second derivatives describe curvature and optimization behavior.
Watch the trap: Applying the ordinary power rule to xˣ treats the changing exponent as constant and gives the wrong result.

What derivatives reveal

Mean value theorem, monotonicity, concavity and graph reasoning

01 · Rolle’s theorem and the mean value theorem

Rolle’s theorem assumes continuity on [a,b], differentiability on (a,b), and equal endpoint values. It guarantees some c inside with f′(c)=0. It does not claim every stationary point has equal endpoints or that the guaranteed point is unique.

The mean value theorem uses the same continuity and differentiability assumptions without equal endpoint values. There exists c with f′(c)=[f(b)−f(a)]/(b−a). Somewhere the instantaneous slope equals the average slope. Apply the theorem only after checking its hypotheses.

If f′=0 throughout an interval, f is constant there. If f′>0 throughout an interval, f is strictly increasing; f′≥0 implies nondecreasing. Strictly increasing functions can have isolated zero derivatives, for example x³ at zero. Do not reverse the sufficient condition carelessly.

A bound |f′|≤M on an interval gives |f(b)−f(a)|≤M|b−a|. This converts a slope bound into an output-change bound and explains why bounded derivatives control sensitivity. Disconnected domains require separate interval reasoning.

A related-rates problem links changing quantities through an equation, then differentiates with respect to time. If area A=πr² and radius depends on time, dA/dt=2πr dr/dt. Units are an excellent consistency check: length times length/time gives area/time.

Taylor series & approximation

01 · Constructing a Taylor polynomial

A Taylor polynomial matches a function and its first n derivatives at a center a: Tₙ(x)=Σₖ₌₀ⁿ f⁽ᵏ⁾(a)(x−a)ᵏ/k!. The constant term matches height, the linear term slope, the quadratic term curvature. Maclaurin means the special center a=0.

The factorial matters because the k-th derivative of (x−a)ᵏ at a is k!. Compute derivatives first, evaluate them at the center, divide by factorials, then attach powers. A Taylor polynomial is finite; a Taylor series is an infinite expression whose convergence and equality to f require analysis.

The standard Maclaurin series are eˣ=Σxᵏ/k!, sin x=x−x³/3!+x⁵/5!−…, and cos x=1−x²/2!+x⁴/4!−…. These converge to their functions for all real x. Parity explains the missing terms for sine and cosine.

For a different center, use h=x−a. For example ln x near a=1 becomes ln(1+h), with powers of h. A polynomial centered near the input often needs fewer terms than one centered far away.

01 · Local tests and global extrema

A local minimum is no larger than nearby domain values; a global minimum is no larger than every value in the domain. A differentiable interior extremum must satisfy f′=0 (Fermat’s theorem). The converse is false: x³ has a stationary point without an extremum.

For a first derivative sign test, a change from negative to positive gives a local minimum, positive to negative a local maximum. No sign change gives neither. If f′(c)=0 and f″(c)>0, c is a strict local minimum; f″(c)<0 gives a strict local maximum; zero is inconclusive.

On a closed bounded interval for continuous f, list endpoints and all interior points where f′=0 or is undefined. Evaluate f at every candidate and compare. Endpoints can win even though their derivatives are nonzero, because the feasible domain is one-sided there.

03 · Gradient descent, Newton steps and stopping

Single-variable gradient descent uses xₖ₊₁=xₖ−ηf′(xₖ) with step size η>0. It moves downhill locally, but a step that is too large can increase the objective or diverge. A small derivative is a stationarity signal, not proof of a global minimum.

For f(x)=½ax² with a>0, iteration becomes xₖ₊₁=(1−ηa)xₖ. It converges to zero exactly when |1−ηa|<1, equivalently 0<η<2/a, except special starting cases. Small steps are slow; intermediate steps can alternate signs while converging.

Newton’s optimization step is xₖ₊₁=xₖ−f′(xₖ)/f″(xₖ), when the denominator is nonzero. It follows a local quadratic approximation and can converge rapidly near a suitable nondegenerate minimum. It can also jump outside the domain, hit zero curvature or approach a maximum; safeguards such as damping matter.

Bisection on f′ can find a stationary point when the derivative is continuous and brackets a sign change. For a strictly convex function that crossing is the unique minimum. In practical iterations, track objective change, step size, derivative magnitude and a maximum iteration count rather than relying on one criterion.

Endpoints and all interior critical candidates. Corners can matter too, and candidates must lie in the domain.

5. Minimum of x+4/x on x>0 is…

  1. 4 at x=2
  2. 2 at x=1
  3. 0 at x=0
  4. Not attained
Answer & reasoning

4 at x=2. Derivative vanishes at 2 and the function is strictly convex on x>0.

6. A differentiable convex function with f′(c)=0 has…

  1. A global maximum at c
  2. No extremum
  3. A unique minimum without further assumptions
  4. A global minimum at c
Answer & reasoning

A global minimum at c. Convexity gives global minimality; strict convexity is needed for guaranteed uniqueness.

7. For f=(x−2)², one GD step from 0 with η=1/4 gives…

  1. −1
  2. 4
  3. 1
  4. 2
Answer & reasoning

1. Derivative at zero is −4, so 0−(1/4)(−4)=1.

8. For f=x², η=1 makes nonzero GD iterates…

  1. Become undefined
  2. Oscillate without shrinking
  3. Converge in one step
  4. Diverge in magnitude
Answer & reasoning

Oscillate without shrinking. The update multiplier is 1−2η=−1.

9. Newton’s optimization update is…

  1. x−f′/f″
  2. x−f/f′
  3. x+f′
  4. x−f″/f′
Answer & reasoning

x−f′/f″. It solves for a zero of the derivative using local curvature.

10. A local minimum is necessarily global?

  1. Yes for every differentiable function
  2. Yes on every bounded domain
  3. Yes if f′=0
  4. No
Answer & reasoning

No. Multiple wells can have different values; convexity provides a useful global guarantee.

Supporting bridge: integration & gradients

Extra preparation for probability, least squares and machine learning

01 · Integration recap: accumulation and area

This is supporting preparation, beyond the explicitly named single-variable DA calculus list. An antiderivative F satisfies F′=f. Indefinite integration gives a family F+C because derivatives erase constants. For p≠−1, ∫xᵖdx=xᵖ⁺¹/(p+1)+C; ∫1/x dx=ln|x|+C on intervals avoiding zero.

The definite integral accumulates signed area. For continuous f on [a,b], the fundamental theorem gives ∫ₐᵇf(x)dx=F(b)−F(a). Parts below the axis contribute negatively. Total geometric area requires integrating |f| or splitting at sign changes.

Substitution reverses the chain rule: ∫f(g(x))g′(x)dx becomes ∫f(u)du with u=g(x). Integration by parts reverses the product rule: ∫u dv=uv−∫v du. Change limits as well when substituting in a definite integral.

A probability density integrates to one, and an expectation integrates a value weighted by its density. These are future applications, not a substitute for studying probability assumptions. Numerical quadrature approximates an integral by weighted samples; its accuracy depends on smoothness and resolution.

∫ₐᵇf=F(b)−F(a) · ∫u dv=uv−∫v du · ∫2xeˣ²dx=eˣ²+C

Work it through

  1. Compute ∫₀¹x²dx. An antiderivative is x³/3.
  2. Evaluate at the bounds: 1/3−0=1/3.
  3. A midpoint sum with n bins uses xᵢ=(i+1/2)/n and width 1/n.
  4. As the mesh shrinks, the sum approaches 1/3.
Application: Accumulated cost, probability mass and expected loss are integrals.
Watch the trap: An indefinite integral needs +C; a definite integral is a number with signed area, not necessarily positive.
for n in [10,100,1000]:

02 · Partial derivatives, gradients and Hessians

This lesson is an application bridge rather than a separately named Section 3 topic. For f(x₁,…,xₙ), a partial derivative changes one coordinate while holding the others fixed. The gradient collects the partial derivatives into a vector; its dot product with a unit direction gives the directional derivative when f is differentiable.

The gradient points in the direction of steepest Euclidean increase when nonzero. Its norm is that maximum directional rate. A zero gradient is necessary at a differentiable unconstrained interior optimum, but it may also occur at a maximum or saddle.

The Hessian collects second partial derivatives. When mixed derivatives are continuous they agree, making the Hessian symmetric. At a stationary point, a positive definite Hessian gives a strict local minimum, a negative definite one a strict maximum, and an indefinite one a saddle. Semidefinite cases need further investigation.

For symmetric A, ∇(½xᵀAx−bᵀx)=Ax−b. For a general A, the gradient of xᵀAx is (A+Aᵀ)x. For least squares ½||Mx−y||², the gradient is Mᵀ(Mx−y), so setting it to zero produces the normal equations studied in Linear Algebra.

∇f=(∂f/∂xᵢ) · Duf=∇fᵀu (||u||=1) · Hᵢⱼ=∂²f/(∂xᵢ∂xⱼ)

Work it through

  1. For f=x²+xy+2y², ∇f=(2x+y,x+4y).
  2. The Hessian is [[2,1],[1,4]], symmetric with positive leading minors 2 and 7.
  3. The unique stationary point is (0,0).
  4. Positive definiteness proves a strict global minimum because the entire function is quadratic.
Application: This is the mathematical connection between matrix least squares and gradient-based learning.
Watch the trap: Positive semidefinite Hessian at a single stationary point does not alone prove a minimum for a general nonlinear function.
import numpy as np

x=np.array([1.,2.])
print('gradient',A@x)
print('curvatures',np.linalg.eigvalsh(A))

03 · A capstone: fit a line and explain the loss

Combine the two tracks by fitting y≈β₀+β₁t to observations. Build a design matrix X with a column of ones and a column of t values. The prediction vector Xβ lies in the column space of X; least squares projects observed y onto that space.

Use L(β)=½||Xβ−y||². The gradient is Xᵀ(Xβ−y), the Hessian XᵀX. When the t values are not all equal, the two columns are independent and the loss has one global minimizer. The normal equations express residual orthogonality.

Solve with numpy.linalg.lstsq and verify both the fitted values and Xᵀr≈0. To explore gradient descent, choose a learning rate below 2/λmax(XᵀX) for this unnormalized quadratic loss. If you divide the loss by sample count, the gradient and safe learning-rate scale change too.

Finish by explaining three viewpoints in your own words: geometric projection, algebraic normal equations and derivative-based stationarity. A strong understanding connects them instead of memorizing them as unrelated formulas. This capstone prepares for regression without claiming to cover the full machine-learning track.

β*=argmin ½||Xβ−y||² · Xᵀr=0 · H=XᵀX

Work it through

  1. Use t=(0,1,2), y=(1,2,2). Then XᵀX=[[3,3],[3,5]] and Xᵀy=(5,6).
  2. Solve 3β₀+3β₁=5 and 3β₀+5β₁=6.
  3. β₁=1/2 and β₀=7/6; predictions are (7/6,5/3,13/6).
  4. Residual y−Xβ=(−1/6,1/3,−1/6); both its sum and t-weighted sum are zero.
Application: A small end-to-end model checks that geometry, calculus and code agree.
Watch the trap: A prediction residual can be nonzero at the optimum; what vanishes is its projection onto every design-column direction.
